# Pedagogy and evidence: claims, interpretations and limits

**Research record, 21–22 October 2026.** This is a bounded design review, not a systematic literature review. Sources were selected for the five requested lenses and direct relevance to aural/instrumental learning. No full inaccessible book, expert interview, classroom trial or causal evaluation of Musikkverkstad is claimed. Bibliographic dates below refer to publications; web access dates refer to this review. Source summaries are deliberately brief; curriculum choices are ours.

## Source register and attributable claims

### S1 — Kulturskolerådet: normative framework

Consulted the official [online rammeplan](https://kulturskoleradet.no/rammeplanen), especially current sections 3.1.2, 3.2.1, 3.4 and 3.5. It supports pupil participation, adapted teaching, progression, constructive feedback, independent artistic choices and critical use of technology. **The same page appends subject plans explicitly labelled 2016–2017.** Their music wheel and instrumental phases are older guidance, not newly written requirements of the framework adopted in 2024. Earlier Theme 03 references to the music section should be read with that distinction.

**Our inference:** connect musical action, choice, reflection and transfer, while considering diverse pupils and contexts. **Boundary:** this is normative guidance, not evidence that an app, four levels or a 38-opportunity plan improves learning. It does not prescribe our calendar.

### S2 — Keith Swanwick: C(L)A(S)P

Original reference: *A Basis for Music Education* (1979). Its full text was not accessible in this review. The model’s five terms were corroborated in Anthony Anderson’s [2021 curriculum-design study, section “Musical pedagogies and curriculum design”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-music-education/article/understanding-curriculum-design-in-the-perceptions-and-practices-of-classroom-music-teachers-in-the-lower-secondary-school-in-england/42F841B9BE179A01AC6995418ECDD204): **Composition, Literature studies, Audition, Skill acquisition, Performance**. This is secondary attribution for Swanwick’s model, not direct full-book reading. Parentheses conventionally distinguish supporting literature/skill work from the central musical experiences of composing, listening and performing; primary-book verification of detailed interpretation remains desirable. The composition reviewer also consulted a search-cached excerpt of Swanwick’s [“Purpose in Music Education”](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203975343-34); direct chapter access failed, so this is not a full-primary-text reading.

**Our inference:** direct listening, playing and inventing should drive encounters, with terminology/context and technique serving them. “Audition” here means musical listening, not an entrance examination. C(L)A(S)P is an organising lens, not five sequential app modes or a test battery. Exact matching covers only part of listening and cannot stand in for expressive understanding. Our spiral is not Swanwick/Tillman’s developmental theory.

### S3 — Edwin Gordon / GIML: audiation and patterns

Read official GIML pages on [audiation](https://giml.org/mlt/audiation/) and [skill learning sequence](https://giml.org/mlt/skilllearningsequence/). The former distinguishes musical comprehension/prediction from immediate sound perception; the latter describes discrimination and inference learning, familiar pattern vocabulary and aural/oral work before symbolic association. These are institutional explanations of Music Learning Theory, not the full Gordon books or independent efficacy studies.

**Our inference:** invite prediction, humming/tapping and familiar-pattern comparison before optional notation, then use retained features in new replies. **Boundary:** a correct echo selection cannot diagnose inner hearing; our four selectable configurations are not Gordon’s hierarchy or validated audiation stages. The source’s strong readiness sequence and our non-linear menu are not identical approaches.

### S4 — John Paynter: composing and creative exploration

Consulted the publisher description of [*Sound and Structure* (1992)](https://books.google.com/books/about/Sound_and_Structure.html?id=qLF5swEACAAJ); no preview/full book was available. It presents creative activity across composing, interpreting and listening. Also read the author’s abstract of [“Making progress with composing” (2000), DOI 10.1017/S0265051700000115](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-music-education/article/abs/making-progress-with-composing/4F0386B2444EC959DB06CCBC523D034E). Paynter argues that difficulties with classroom composing raise questions about supporting musical progress rather than simply discrediting composing. Abstract access does not establish the article’s complete argument or evidence.

**Our inference:** let pupils make, hear and revise ideas from the beginning, rather than treating invention as a reward after drills. A fixed-rhythm editor is a useful constrained resource but cannot represent all creative exploration. **Boundary:** no Paynter project, empirical result or chapter-specific sequence is reconstructed from unavailable pages.

### S5 — Lucy Green: informal learning, not merely a free menu

Read the author’s [Open University interview transcript](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/mod/oucontent/view.php?id=47959&section=3.4) and relevant sections of the deposited [“The music curriculum as lived experience” manuscript](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1479473/1/Green2005Music27.pdf). These describe learning by ear, chosen familiar music, individual/peer processes, integrated musical activity and non-linear routes. The manuscript reports early, contextual classroom work and mixed pupil preferences about help. Its repository filename/header are not a verified final bibliographic citation; cite this accessible manuscript directly. *Music, Informal Learning and the School* was not read in full.

**Our inference:** offer ear-led entry, optional help and pupil-chosen revision/partner exchange. **Boundary:** supplied piano miniatures and selectable levels do not reproduce friendship groups, chosen recordings or whole-song learning. Teacher observation/guidance remains significant; this is not a justification for withdrawing help.

### S6 — empirical lead: ear playing and instrumental learning

Read Baker and Green’s [2013 UCL record](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10123257/) and accepted manuscript’s assessment and limitations sections, [DOI 10.1177/1321103X13508254](https://doi.org/10.1177/1321103X13508254). Sixteen matched pairs, aged 10–14, compared ear-playing and conventional instrumental approaches over seven to ten weeks. The authors report encouraging aural results and call for larger, longer replication; selection and pianist predominance limit generalisation. Their “closure” criterion concerned completing/recovering a played response, not simply ending on the tonic.

**Our inference:** include genuine played responses and transfer observations in evaluation. **Boundary:** a small taught instrumental study does not validate solo browser quizzes, annual outcomes, automatic assessment or our age/level design. Do not import its rating scales as app grades, claim all skills improved significantly, or treat its closure result as evidence for Theme 03’s named-C ending task.

## Synthesis without pretending the theories agree

| Tension | Design judgement for this application | Risk to monitor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structured pattern readiness versus personal non-linear routes | Provide short explicit scaffolds alongside direct creation and optional entry. | Neither unsupported exploration nor compulsory drill completion is acceptable. This compromise is our choice, not a unified validated theory. |
| Constrained assessment versus composing/interpretation | Mark a taught event relation when objective; discuss effect and intention without aesthetic scoring. | An objective correct answer must not imply other replies are bad music. |
| Short solo miniatures versus music in social/cultural context | Use manageable examples but connect to playable phrases, partners and contextual listening. | Context cannot be supplied by decorative genre labels or an invented account of a tradition. |
| Useful progression versus developmental determinism | Revisit relations with greater agency and optional complexity. | Age, theme number, calendar spiral and selected level must not become a single ability ladder. |
| Accessible notation/labels versus musical hearing | Offer symbols after/alongside sound as aids, not compulsory literacy. | A puzzle solved entirely through labels may provide little evidence of the target hearing. |

## Evidence claims permitted now

We can say the proposal is **informed by** these sources and has undergone non-human specialist-style critique. We cannot say it is proven effective, endorsed by those authors/institutions, human-specialist approved or age-validated. No numerical session length, level threshold, pilot size or annual allocation is derived as an optimal prescription from these sources.

Before a learning-effect claim: define a narrow outcome, observe a diverse sample using actual musical performance as well as on-screen behaviour, document support/context, compare alternatives where appropriate, and report uncertainty. A formative pilot finds design problems; it does not establish population efficacy. Before pupil release, the separate musical, browser/audio, physical-device/accessibility and exact-candidate release evidence remains required.
